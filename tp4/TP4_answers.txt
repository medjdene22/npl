TP4 — Lab 4: Stopword Removal
Written answers
==============================

Exercise 1 — Identifying stopwords

Tasks
- Stopwords in the sentence: the, is, on, a.
- Complement: student, working, natural, language, processing, project, "."
  A human can still guess the topic (an NLP project) from these content words.
- natural, language and processing are not stopwords. That is expected: they are
  content words. Note that "processing" is still generic inside an NLP corpus
  (see Exercise 4).
- First 15 words: a, about, above, after, again, against, ain, all, am, an, and,
  any, are, aren, aren't.
  Categories:
    articles/determiners: a, an
    prepositions: about, above, after, against
    adverbs/conjunctions: again, all, any
    be/auxiliaries: am, are, aren't, ain

Questions
Q1. Recognised stopwords: the, is, on, a.
Q2. No. "not" is very frequent but flips the meaning of a sentiment sentence, and
    "is" can be the focus of a question-answering query.
Q3. It depends on the task. Removal helps when word identity matters more than
    grammar (search, topic classification) and hurts when small words carry
    meaning (sentiment, QA, translation).


Exercise 2 — Quantifying the effect

Tasks
1. The Exercise 1 sentence goes from 11 tokens to 7; the, is, on, a are removed.
2. stopword_stats() returns original, remaining, removed and percentage removed.
3. Results:
     style      original  kept  removed  % removed
     tweet            14     8        6      42.9%
     formal           15     7        8      53.3%
     keywords          5     5        0       0.0%
4. The percentage varies a lot (0-53%). A keyword list has almost no function
   words, formal prose is dense in them, and a tweet is in between. Style matters
   more than length.


Exercise 3 — The importance of context: negation

Tasks
1. After NLTK stopword removal:
     "This movie is not good." -> movie good .
     "This movie is really not that bad, actually." -> movie really bad , actually .
2. The negation word removed in both cases is "not". In sentence 2, "that" is
   also a stopword, but it is not the negator.
3. Naive scores:
     sentence 1: +1 before removal, +1 after removal
     sentence 2: -1 before removal, -1 after removal
   Removal changes nothing because the scorer ignores "not" - that is the real
   weakness.
4. Fix: keep not/no/never/n't/nor and flip the polarity when a negator appears
   within two tokens before a sentiment word. Scores become:
     "not good" -> -1
     "not ... bad" -> +1
   This is much more sensible.

Questions
Q1. Negation/polarity disappears: "not good" becomes "good".
Q2. Before the fix, "not" was dropped by the NLTK list and ignored by the naive
    scorer, so the scores stayed +1/-1. After the fix it is kept and flips the
    polarity.
Q3. Sentiment depends on small words such as not, no, never, very and really.
    Blindly removing them can invert or weaken the polarity.


Exercise 4 — Custom, domain-specific stopword lists

Tasks
1. NLTK has 198 words, the custom list has 4. 194 words are in NLTK but not in
   the custom list; nothing is in custom but not NLTK (it is a subset).
2. Raw frequencies (punctuation ignored): processing 4, language 3, text 3;
   every other word appears once.
3. Extension: add processing, language and text. They are frequent in this
   corpus but weak at telling documents apart in an NLP course.
4. Vocabulary: NLTK only leaves 22 distinct words; NLTK + domain leaves 19.
   The words removed by the extension are exactly language, processing, text.

Question
Q. A generic list is domain-independent. It cannot know that "processing" is noise
   in an NLP corpus, or that "not" is critical for sentiment. A domain list adapts
   the removal to the corpus and the task.


Exercise 5 — Integrated preprocessing mini-pipeline

Stages
1. RegEx: mask the URL as <URL> and the price as <PRICE>.
2. Tokenize with word_tokenize.
3. Normalize: lowercase; 2018 and 1,000 become <NUM>.
4. Stopword removal: use NLTK's list but keep the negators. "n't" is preserved.
   Why: "Isn't that amazing?" is a positive rhetorical question, and Exercise 3
   showed that dropping negation can flip sentiment. NLTK does not list "n't",
   but "is" is removed, so the negation cue survives on purpose.
5. Token counts:
     raw                           43
     after RegEx                   40
     after tokenization            40
     after normalization           40
     after stopword removal        30
   The raw count is higher because the URL is split into several tokens before
   it is masked.
